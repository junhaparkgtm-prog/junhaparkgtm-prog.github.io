01
The problem

Three failures nobody could see.

Every one of these was invisible in a dashboard. They only show up when you go read the data yourself.

01

Wrong call order

The CRM sorts by most recent activity. But recency doesn’t predict intent — tags do. Contacts marked booked, hot lead, or replied convert at a different rate than newsletter subscribers, and sorting by date buries them.

02

Collision risk

Two tools sending to overlapping lists with no shared suppression. A prospect mid-conversation with one rep can receive a cold sequence from another the same week — and nothing in either system flags it.

03

Silent decay

Contacts who raised a hand and were never followed up on. No system surfaced them, so they aged out quietly. The acquisition cost was already spent; the intent signal just expired.

02
How I built it

A scoring layer over two systems that don’t talk.

Orchestration

Python over the CRM and the sender, not inside either one

Neither tool could see the other, and neither exposed the logic I needed. So the pipeline lives outside both: pull full exports, resolve identity across systems, score and segment in pandas, then write clean lists back with tags that survive in the client’s CRM rather than in a spreadsheet only I can read.

Extract & normalize

Full exports from both systems. Normalized email casing, extracted domains, matched contacts on exact email and shared domain. Dropped invalid addresses and records with no identifiable person.

Score

Weighted scoring on fit, engagement, and recency. Tags carry the heaviest weight — they proved to be the only reliable signal, since company name was null on 58% of records.

Segment

Every qualified contact sorted into exactly one of four sequences: no-show rebook, closed-lost, lead magnet re-engagement, long-term dormant. No contact appears twice.

Reconcile

The step most reactivation work skips. Exported all 45 live campaigns, merged and deduplicated them, then cross-matched against every segment. Anyone already in an active campaign was pulled before launch.

Suppress

Rebuilt the do-not-email list from bounces, unsubscribes, opt-outs, and everyone currently in a live campaign — from 615 records to 1,570.

Attribute

Every reactivated contact tagged at sequence entry with sequence name and date, so source attribution lives in the CRM permanently instead of in my export.

04
Design decisions

Why it’s built this way.

01

Tags over recency

Every CRM defaults to sorting by date. In this dataset, date told you almost nothing and tags told you almost everything. The scoring model reflects the data, not the convention.

02

Reconcile before send

Most suppression is reactive — you find out you double-sent when someone complains. Pulling the live campaign list first makes the error preventable instead of forgivable.

03

Small-business email isn’t low-quality email

An early version of the filter penalized shared inboxes and free domains. For small operators, admissions@ is the owner and gmail is the business address. Scoring was corrected to stop burying the segment most likely to convert.

What it proves

Revenue systems fail when they can’t remember state.

My flagship outbound engine’s hardest problem was idempotency — making sure a run never re-touched a prospect it had already reached. The first thing I found in this CRM was the same failure wearing different clothes: two tools with no shared memory, about to contact the same seven people twice.

That’s the throughline in how I build. The interesting part of a GTM system is rarely the sending. It’s the state management underneath it — knowing who you’ve touched, what they did, and what that means for the next run. Get that wrong and every clever thing on top of it reads as spam.

This project also isn’t a build for its own sake. The diagnosis came first: read the data, find where revenue was leaking, quantify it, then write only the code that fixed it.
