GTM thesis How I think · 2026

GTM is a leverage problem.

Most revenue teams scale by adding reps. That's the expensive way. I think go-to-market works the way wealth does: you win with specific knowledge about your buyer, leverage that doesn't need permission, and judgment about where a human still matters. Below are the twelve plays I'd run at any company, and where I've already run them.

01

ICP definition

Your ICP is who stays and pays, not who replies.

Most companies have a rough sense of who buys and nothing data-driven behind it. Reply rate is the trap: the segment that answers fastest is often the one that churns fastest. The better question is retroactive. Which customers closed, stayed longest, and grew?

With enough closed-won history in the CRM, run that analysis on deals that have lived their full lifecycle. Without it, test two or three candidate ICPs in parallel through outbound and let the market tell you which one feels the problem.

Where I've run it

At Atomic Enrollment the answer got specific: owners of CDL, HVAC, welding, and cosmetology schools, often former tradespeople, who make fast decisions and buy filled seats, not marketing. That one definition shaped everything downstream, from list sources to the words in every video.

02

Account scoring

Two scores, not one. Fit decides if you track an account. Signals decide if you call it today.

Firmographic fit (industry, size, location) barely moves, so it sets the tier. Signals decay fast, so they set the priority. For the model itself I prefer the middle path: a deterministic equation the agent runs, with the agent writing out its reasoning so a rep can see why an account scored a 7 and argue with it. A black-box score nobody trusts is a score nobody uses.

Tier 1Several touchpoints across several channels
Tier 2A call plus a rep-written email
Tier 3Automated outbound sequence
02b

Calibrating the score

Before trusting any score, hand-pick 10 to 20 accounts that are perfect fits and 10 to 20 you'd never sell to. Run the model against both. Every perfect fit that scores low, and every bad fit that scores high, is a rule you haven't written yet.

03

Signals

Be precise about which signals you track and exactly what each one is worth.

"Signals" got sold as a black box: somebody at this company might be interested in something like your product. The value isn't in having signals. It's in the weighting. A visit to your pricing page beats a funding announcement every time, because it's further down the funnel. And the best signals are the ones built for your market that nobody else is watching.

03b

Signals I'd weight for trade schools

Active ad spend
Already in my enrichment

A school running paid ads is already paying for leads. If there's no instant follow-up on the site, that money is leaking, and it's visible from outside.

Admissions hiring
Custom signal

A job post for an admissions rep or enrollment coordinator is a school announcing that follow-up is breaking. The description tells you how.

Video rewatches
Highest intent in the motion

A prospect rewatching the video made for their school is the hottest signal I have. The play is a call within minutes, not days.

Champion moves
Warmest outbound there is

Every owner and director who's had a good result with us, tracked for role changes. Happy at the last school means a warm call at the next one.

04

List building

Build the list where your buyer actually lives.

If they live on LinkedIn, scrape LinkedIn. Local businesses start from maps data, then waterfall enrichment to find the owner. Ecom has its own databases. The underrated category is regulated industries, where the government already maintains the list for you. When none of those fit, raw web search plus a scraper Claude writes beats buying a stale vendor list.

Where I've run it

Trade school owners mostly don't live on LinkedIn, and they're a public-record industry: CDL schools register with the FMCSA Training Provider Registry, cosmetology schools with state boards. My engine sources in Prospeo, qualifies each school's site against the ICP with Firecrawl, and only then spends enrichment credits. Qualify before you pay.

05

Fully automated outbound

Automate the research and the asset. Keep the copy on a template.

Full automation works when ACVs are lower and the prospect pool is big. Two rules I don't bend. Deliverability comes first: real, warmed Google and Microsoft inboxes, nothing shared with strangers who can burn the server. And copy isn't written agentically. A template with variables is the only way to know which message is actually working.

Where I've run it

CVP Engine: a Claude Code agent wiring Prospeo, Firecrawl, Claude, Modal, Bunny CDN, and Instantly. It sources decision makers, researches each school's site and ads, renders a personalized video, and ships the sequence. 25 to 50 videos a day on about 30 minutes of my time, and replies feed the next run. 06

Man in the loop

When the deal is big enough, the agent drafts and the human sends.

Same sourcing, same enrichment, same research as full automation. The difference is the last mile: a rep edits every message before it goes. Higher ACVs and tier 1 accounts deserve a person's judgment on the words, and the system's job is to make that person faster, not replace them.

Where I've run it

Enterprise at Filevine meant multithreading into 1,000+ attorney firms, where one wrong line burns the account. At Atomic, every contact I dial loads into the CRM with a brief already built: business model, how they enroll today, where the funnel breaks, an opening line, and three pain points specific to their niche. I never walk into a call cold.

Convert

07

Inbound forms + routing

Every field you ask for costs you conversions. Let enrichment fill in the rest.

With waterfall enrichment near full coverage, a form can be name and work email. The gap between 70% and near-100% coverage is the gap between knowing who your leads are and guessing. Route inside the CRM, and route after enrichment, not before, or you're routing on half the picture.

Where I've run it

In enrollment, speed decides it. Students shop three to five schools and usually go with whoever follows up first. Atomic's offer is built around that: Ivy, an AI sales rep, works a school's inquiries the moment they land, and I built the speed-to-lead infrastructure on our own side.

08

Meeting automation

No rep should walk into a call cold, or leave one without the CRM updated.

Before the call: past recordings plus enrichment become a prep brief. After the call: an agent connected to the CRM updates the record and drafts the follow-up. A good Claude skill covers most of it. Hosting it so it fires automatically before every meeting is the upgrade.

Built, and what I'm wiring next

The before half is how I already dial. The after half is next: call recorder transcript into the CRM record, with the follow-up drafted from what the prospect actually said, not what I remember an hour later.

09

Proposal drafting

Write the proposal from the call, not from the template.

Past proposals plus house rules become a skill on every rep's Claude. The discovery transcript goes in, a draft doc comes out. The rep's job shifts from writing to judging, which is where their time should go anyway.

A rule I learned closing

Pricing follows the debrief, not the script. What the buyer said on the call should shape the offer, and a proposal skill should be built to enforce that, not paper over it.

Compound
every touch worth more next time
10

ABM asset generation

The strongest outbound message is an asset only that account could receive.

For tier 1, build something for one company: a video, a one-pager, a landing page, a teardown of their funnel. The best setup is a skill that reads your sales materials and company context, so every asset sounds like you instead of like a template.

Where I've run it

This is the whole CVP motion: a custom video for every school, built from its own website. The play predates the automation. Run by hand, it drove $1.87M in revenue and 97 enrollments for one heavy equipment training school. I automated it. On the collateral side I render one-pagers, decks, and closer SOPs in HTML on the brand system.

11

Event plays

Events are the rare moment your buyer tells you where they'll be.

Virtual events: follow up on the topic of the event, not your product. In person: scrape every post mentioning the event, have Claude keep only the ones that confirm attending, qualify the people who posted, then send one line. Want to meet up there?

How I'd apply it

School owners gather at industry conferences and accreditation events, but most of them aren't posting about it. So the list comes from exhibitor and speaker pages instead of social posts. Same play, different source.

12

CRM hygiene + enrichment

Your CRM is the cheapest pipeline you'll ever have, if you can trust it.

Years of stale titles, bounced emails, and empty fields make every other play on this page worse. A one-time cleanup goes stale within a year, so enrichment has to run continuously. Start with the segment you care least about, prove the process, then work up to what matters most.

Where I've run it

At Atomic I rebuilt an 8,000+ contact master list into the CRM with clean fields and notes, then built the Reactivation Engine on top: five sequences covering no-shows, closed-lost, and long-dormant leads. The database you already paid for is net-new pipeline if you work it. See the build

The loop

Twelve feeds one.

A clean CRM is what makes the retroactive ICP analysis possible. The system closes on itself, and every lap it knows the buyer a little better.

That's the part I care about most. Not any single play, but the compounding. Each list is sharper than the last, each score better calibrated, each message closer to what the buyer actually said.

It's the same way I think about a career. Build specific knowledge nobody can hand you, point leverage at it, and let it compound. GTM is just where I get to do that every day, with a number attached.
